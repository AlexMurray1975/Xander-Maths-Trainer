Why Big Ocean States

Small Island Developing States steward 30% of the world’s ocean through their exclusive economic zones, and receive seven times less finance than least developed countries and eleven times less than other lower-middle-income countries. Outrigger invests where that gap is widest.

The opportunity

A blue economy on course to double, and the states that cannot finance it

The global blue economy is valued at $1.5 trillion a year and is expected to reach $3 trillion by 2030. Big Ocean States are central to it, and structurally excluded from financing it. Life Below Water remains the least funded of the seventeen Sustainable Development Goals: less than 2% of philanthropic and official development assistance funding supports ocean industries.

The capital problem

Why conventional capital does not fit

The shortfall in island states is usually described as a lack of appetite. It is more accurately a mismatch of design. Investment structures built for continental emerging markets meet a set of conditions in island economies that they were not drawn for, and the projects fail the process long before anyone reaches a view on the businesses themselves.

  • Scale

    Island economies are small, and so are the projects within them. A port upgrade, a cold chain, a waste recovery facility: individually these sit below the minimum commitment of the funds that finance emerging market infrastructure, while costing much the same to assess as a transaction ten times larger. The consequence is not that these projects fail the tests. It is that they are never put through them.

    The gap has a shape. Between roughly US$2 million and US$10 million, a project has outgrown grant funding but is still too small for the institutions that finance infrastructure, which decline small-scale adaptation work because the operating cost and time it takes do not fall in proportion to the cheque. It is commonly called the valley of death, and it is where most island businesses stop.

  • Distance

    A single national market may be a dozen inhabited islands separated by hundreds of kilometres of open ocean. That multiplies the cost of construction, logistics, maintenance and supervision, and it defeats the assumption of a contiguous domestic market on which most infrastructure models rest.

  • Dependency

    Projects rarely stand alone. Processing needs reliable power; cold storage needs power and water; a landing site needs road access and a functioning port. In a larger market those are given. Here they frequently have to be built or secured as part of the same transaction, which widens the scope and lengthens the timetable.

  • Preparation

    Feasibility work, environmental and social studies, permitting and financial structuring are what turn a proposition into something investable, and in these markets that work is largely unfunded: too commercial for grant makers, too early for investors. It is the most common reason a viable island business never reaches a term sheet, and it is the gap the Technical Assistance Facility exists to close.

  • Sovereign capacity

    A small administration carries the same regulatory, permitting and procurement functions as a large one, with a fraction of the staff. Approvals take longer, not because the intent is absent, but because the same handful of officials are handling every project in the country at once.

  • Currency

    Revenues are frequently earned in local currency while capital equipment, fuel and imported inputs are priced in dollars. Hedging instruments are thin or absent in markets of this size, so the mismatch is carried rather than managed, and it is priced into the cost of capital.

  • Climate exposure

    These are among the most climate-exposed economies in the world. Insurance is expensive where it is available at all, a single cyclone season can remove a year of output, and the concentration of most island economies in one or two sectors means that exposure is rarely diversified away. Diversifying within the blue economy is itself a form of adaptation.

Capital built for islands, not adapted to them

None of these constraints is an argument against investing in Big Ocean States. Each is an argument against arriving with an instrument designed somewhere else. What the geography needs is project preparation funded before the investment decision rather than after it, structures sized to the market as it actually is, public and philanthropic capital used where the risk profile genuinely requires it, and origination done on the islands themselves rather than at a distance from them.

The eligible geography

Thirty-five states, three regions, one strategy

Outrigger’s eligible geography is the group of states recognised as Small Island Developing States by the United Nations, with two deliberate adjustments. Singapore, Haiti, Cuba and Bahrain are excluded. Sri Lanka is included, because it faces the same set of challenges. That leaves thirty-five states across three ocean regions, holding between them exclusive economic zones that cover roughly a third of the world’s ocean.

These states are home to some 65 million people, 20% of global biodiversity and 40% of the world’s coral reefs.

How to read it

The circle is centred on the state and drawn to the correct area: it shows how much ocean, not its shape. Several of these zones are island groups spread across thousands of kilometres, so the real boundary is nothing like a circle. States sit at their true longitude and latitude on a schematic equirectangular projection centred on the Pacific. No maritime boundaries are drawn.
